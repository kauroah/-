<p>A <b>company</b> is a <a href="/wiki/Legal_personality" class="mw-redirect" title="Legal personality">legal entity</a> representing an association of <a href="/wiki/Legal_person" title="Legal person">legal persons</a> with a shared objective, such as generating profit or benefiting society. Depending on the <a href="/wiki/Jurisdiction" title="Jurisdiction">jurisdiction</a>, companies can take on various forms, including <a href="/wiki/Voluntary_association" title="Voluntary association">voluntary associations</a>, <a href="/wiki/Nonprofit_organization" title="Nonprofit organization">nonprofit organizations</a>, <a href="/wiki/List_of_legal_entity_types_by_country" title="List of legal entity types by country">business entities</a>, financial entities, <a href="/wiki/Bank" title="Bank">banks</a>, and <a href="/wiki/Educational_institution" title="Educational institution">educational institutions</a>. Companies have evolved to share common legal features, such as a <a href="/wiki/Corporate_personhood" title="Corporate personhood">separate legal personality</a>, <a href="/wiki/Limited_liability" title="Limited liability">limited liability</a>, <a href="/wiki/Share_(finance)" title="Share (finance)">transferable shares</a>, <a href="/wiki/Equity_(finance)" title="Equity (finance)">investor ownership</a>, and a <a href="/wiki/Management" title="Management">managerial hierarchy</a>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-:0&#95;1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-1"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>1<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>The term "company" may or may not be synonymous with <a href="/wiki/Corporation" title="Corporation">corporation</a>, <a href="/wiki/Partnership" title="Partnership">partnership</a>, firm, or <a href="/wiki/Society" title="Society">society</a>. Companies are governed by <a href="/wiki/Corporate_law" title="Corporate law">company law</a>, also referred to as corporate law in many regions. Incorporated companies are created through <a href="/wiki/State_(polity)" title="State (polity)">state</a> registration,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-:0&#95;1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-1"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>1<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> whereas <a href="/wiki/Unincorporated_association" title="Unincorporated association">unincorporated companies</a> are not.
</p><p>When a company closes, it may undergo <a href="/wiki/Liquidation" title="Liquidation">liquidation</a> to settle its affairs and terminate its legal obligations. Additionally, companies may associate to form <a href="/wiki/Corporate_group" title="Corporate group">corporate groups</a>, which are collections of parent and subsidiary corporations.
</p>

<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="History">History</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Company&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: History"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1320445320" /><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable">See also: <a href="/wiki/History_of_company_law" class="mw-redirect" title="History of company law">History of company law</a>, <a href="/wiki/History_of_company_law_in_the_United_Kingdom" title="History of company law in the United Kingdom">History of company law in the United Kingdom</a>, and <a href="/wiki/History_of_corporate_law_in_the_United_States" title="History of corporate law in the United States">History of corporate law in the United States</a></div><p>English law recognised long ago that a corporation would have <a href="/wiki/Capacity_(law)" title="Capacity (law)">separate legal personality</a>, also known as <a href="/wiki/Corporate_personhood" title="Corporate personhood">corporate personality</a> or <a href="/wiki/Juridical_person" title="Juridical person">juridical personhood</a>. In 1612, <a href="/wiki/Sir_Edward_Coke" class="mw-redirect" title="Sir Edward Coke">Sir Edward Coke</a> remarked in the <i><a href="/wiki/Case_of_Sutton%27s_Hospital" title="Case of Sutton&#39;s Hospital">Case of Sutton's Hospital</a></i>,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>2<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></p><blockquote><p>the Corporation itself is onely <i>in abstracto</i>, and resteth onely in intendment and consideration of the <a href="/wiki/Law" title="Law">Law</a>; for a Corporation aggregate of many is <a href="/wiki/Invisible" class="mw-redirect" title="Invisible">invisible</a>, <a href="/wiki/Immortality" title="Immortality">immortal</a>, &amp; resteth only in intendment and consideration of the Law; and therefore it cannot have predecessor nor successor. They may not commit <a href="/wiki/Treason" title="Treason">treason</a>, nor be outlawed, nor <a href="/wiki/Excommunicate" class="mw-redirect" title="Excommunicate">excommunicate</a>, for they have no <a href="/wiki/Soul" title="Soul">souls</a>, neither can they appear in person, but by <a href="/wiki/Lawyer" title="Lawyer">Attorney</a>. A Corporation aggregate of many cannot do <a href="/wiki/Fealty" title="Fealty">fealty</a>, for an invisible body cannot be in person, nor can swear, it is not subject to imbecilities, or death of the natural, <a href="/wiki/Human_body" title="Human body">body</a>, and divers other cases.</p></blockquote><p>In 1776, <a href="/wiki/Adam_Smith" title="Adam Smith">Adam Smith</a> wrote in the <i><a href="/wiki/Wealth_of_Nations" class="mw-redirect" title="Wealth of Nations">Wealth of Nations</a></i> that mass corporate activity could not match private entrepreneurship, because people in charge of "other people's money" would not exercise as much care as they would with their own.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>3<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>In 1843, <a href="/wiki/William_Ewart_Gladstone" title="William Ewart Gladstone">William Gladstone</a> took chairmanship of a Parliamentary Committee on Joint Stock Companies, which led to the <a href="/wiki/Joint_Stock_Companies_Act_1844" title="Joint Stock Companies Act 1844">Joint Stock Companies Act 1844</a>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>4<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="United_States">United States</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Company&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: United States"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>At the end of the 19th century in the United States, the law allowed for the concentration of wealth and power in the hands of a few people, or a single person. In response, the <a href="/wiki/Sherman_Antitrust_Act" title="Sherman Antitrust Act">Sherman Antitrust Act</a> of 1890 was created to break up big business conglomerates, and the <a href="/wiki/Clayton_Act" class="mw-redirect" title="Clayton Act">Clayton Act</a> of 1914 gave the government power to halt <a href="/wiki/Mergers_and_acquisitions" title="Mergers and acquisitions">mergers and acquisitions</a> that could damage the public interest. By the end of the <a href="/wiki/First_World_War" class="mw-redirect" title="First World War">First World War</a>, it was increasingly perceived that ordinary people had little voice compared to the "financial oligarchy" of bankers and industrial magnates.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>5<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> In particular, employees lacked voice compared to shareholders, but plans for a post-war "<a href="/wiki/Industrial_democracy" title="Industrial democracy">industrial democracy</a>" (giving employees votes for investing their labor) did not become widespread.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>6<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>The <a href="/wiki/Wall_Street_crash" class="mw-redirect" title="Wall Street crash">Wall Street crash of 1929</a> saw the total collapse of stock market values, as shareholders realized that corporations had become overpriced. They sold shares <i>en masse</i>, meaning many companies found it hard to get finance. The result was that thousands of businesses were forced to close, and they laid off workers. Because workers had less money to spend, businesses received less income, leading to more closures and lay-offs. This downward spiral began the <a href="/wiki/Great_Depression" title="Great Depression">Great Depression</a>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>7<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> This led directly to the <a href="/wiki/New_Deal" title="New Deal">New Deal</a> reforms of the <a href="/wiki/Securities_Act_of_1933" title="Securities Act of 1933">Securities Act of 1933</a> and <a href="/wiki/Securities_and_Exchange_Act_of_1934" class="mw-redirect" title="Securities and Exchange Act of 1934">Securities and Exchange Act of 1934</a>. A new <a href="/wiki/Securities_and_Exchange_Commission" class="mw-redirect" title="Securities and Exchange Commission">Securities and Exchange Commission</a> was empowered to require corporations disclose all material information about their business to the investing public.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>8<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>After <a href="/wiki/World_War_II" title="World War II">World War II</a>, a general consensus emerged that directors were not bound purely to pursue "<a href="/wiki/Shareholder_value" title="Shareholder value">shareholder value</a>" but could exercise their discretion for the good of all stakeholders, for instance by increasing wages instead of dividends, or providing services for the good of the community instead of only pursuing profits, if it was in the interests of the enterprise as a whole.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>9<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> However, different states had different corporate laws. To increase revenue from <a href="/wiki/Corporate_tax" title="Corporate tax">corporate tax</a>, individual states had an incentive to lower their standards in a "<a href="/wiki/Race_to_the_bottom" title="Race to the bottom">race to the bottom</a>" to attract corporations to set up their headquarters in the state, particularly where directors controlled the decision to incorporate. "<a href="/wiki/Charter_competition" class="mw-redirect" title="Charter competition">Charter competition</a>", by the 1960s, had led Delaware to become home to the majority of the largest US corporations. This meant that the case law of the <a href="/wiki/Delaware_Chancery_Court" class="mw-redirect" title="Delaware Chancery Court">Delaware Chancery</a> and <a href="/wiki/Delaware_Supreme_Court" title="Delaware Supreme Court">Supreme Court</a> became increasingly influential.
</p><p>During the 1980s, a huge takeover and merger boom decreased directors' accountability. To fend off a takeover, courts allowed boards to institute "<a href="/wiki/Shareholder_rights_plan" title="Shareholder rights plan">poison pills</a>" or "<a href="/wiki/Shareholder_rights_plan" title="Shareholder rights plan">shareholder rights plans</a>", which allowed directors to veto any bid – and probably get a payout for letting a takeover happen. More and more people's retirement savings were being invested into the stock market, through <a href="/wiki/Pension_fund" title="Pension fund">pension funds</a>, <a href="/wiki/Life_insurance" title="Life insurance">life insurance</a> and <a href="/wiki/Mutual_fund" title="Mutual fund">mutual funds</a>. This resulted in a vast growth in the <a href="/wiki/Asset_management" title="Asset management">asset management</a> industry, which tended to take control of voting rights. Both the financial sector's share of income, and executive pay for chief executive officers began to rise far beyond real wages for the rest of the workforce. The <a href="/wiki/Enron_scandal" title="Enron scandal">Enron scandal</a> of 2001 led to some reforms in the <a href="/wiki/Sarbanes-Oxley_Act" class="mw-redirect" title="Sarbanes-Oxley Act">Sarbanes-Oxley Act</a> (on separating auditors from consultancy work). The <a href="/wiki/2008_financial_crisis" title="2008 financial crisis">2008 financial crisis</a> led to minor changes in the <a href="/wiki/Dodd-Frank_Act" class="mw-redirect" title="Dodd-Frank Act">Dodd-Frank Act</a> (on soft regulation of pay, alongside <a href="/wiki/Derivative" title="Derivative">derivative</a> markets). However, the basic shape of corporate law in the United States has remained the same since the 1980s.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Terminology_and_definition">Terminology and definition</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Company&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Terminology and definition"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>A company can be defined as an "artificial person", invisible, intangible, created by or under law,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>10<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> with a discrete <a href="/wiki/Legal_capacity" class="mw-redirect" title="Legal capacity">legal capacity</a> (or "personality"), <a href="/wiki/Perpetual_succession" title="Perpetual succession">perpetual succession</a>, and a <a href="/wiki/Company_seal" title="Company seal">common seal</a>. Except for some senior positions, companies remain unaffected by the death, insanity, or <a href="/wiki/Insolvency" title="Insolvency">insolvency</a> of an individual member.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="Etymology">Etymology</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Company&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Etymology"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>The English word, "<i>company</i>", has its origins in the <a href="/wiki/Old_French" title="Old French">Old French</a> term <span title="Old French (842-ca. 1400)-language text"><i lang="fro">compagnie</i></span> (first recorded in 1150), meaning "society, friendship, intimacy; body of soldiers",<sup id="cite&#95;ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>11<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> which came from the <a href="/wiki/Late_Latin" title="Late Latin">Late Latin</a> word <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">companio</i></span> ("one who eats bread with you"), first attested in the <a href="/wiki/Salic_law" title="Salic law">Salic law</a> (<abbr title="circa">c.</abbr> AD 500) as a <a href="/wiki/Calque" title="Calque">calque</a> of the <a href="/wiki/Germanic_languages" title="Germanic languages">Germanic</a> expression <span title="Old Frankish-language text"><i lang="frk">gahlaibo</i></span> (literally, "with bread"), related to <a href="/wiki/Old_High_German" title="Old High German">Old High German</a> <span title="Old High German (ca. 750-1050)-language text"><i lang="goh">galeipo</i></span> ("companion") and to <a href="/wiki/Gothic_language" title="Gothic language">Gothic</a> <span title="Gothic-language text"><i lang="got">gahlaiba</i></span> ("messmate").
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="Semantics_and_usage">Semantics and usage</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Company&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Semantics and usage"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>By 1303, the word company referred to <a href="/wiki/Guild" title="Guild">trade guilds</a>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>12<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> The usage of the term <i>company</i> to mean "business association" was first recorded in 1553,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>13<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> and the abbreviation "co." dates from 1769.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>14<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup><sup id="cite&#95;ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>15<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>In <a href="/wiki/English_law" title="English law">English law</a>, a company is a body corporate or corporation company registered under the <a href="/wiki/United_Kingdom_company_law" title="United Kingdom company law">Companies Acts</a> or under similar legislation.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-UK&#95;Companies&#95;Act&#95;2006&#95;16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-UK_Companies_Act_2006-16"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>16<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="Nomenclature">Nomenclature</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Company&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Nomenclature"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<ul><li>Limited and unlimited companies
<ul><li>A limited company is a "company in which the liability of each shareholder is limited to the amount individually invested".<sup id="cite&#95;ref-Black&#39;s&#95;17-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Black&#39;s-17"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>17<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> An unlimited company is a company with no limit on the liability of its members.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-Companies&#95;House&#95;18-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Companies_House-18"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>18<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></li></ul></li>
<li>Private and public companies
<ul><li>A public company is a company whose <a href="/wiki/Share_(finance)" title="Share (finance)">shares</a> may be traded publicly.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-Companies&#95;House&#95;18-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Companies_House-18"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>18<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></li></ul></li></ul>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Types">Types</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Company&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: Types"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
